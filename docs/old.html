Rippled bowl

By key — Camelot wheel · circle of fifths

Read it by colour, like a DJ. Each number owns a colour all the way round the rainbow — neighbours are neighbouring colours (an easy, harmonious step), relative A/B keys share the same colour family (bright outer major, deeper inner minor), and a clash sits opposite in a contrasting colour (8 → 2, blue → orange). If the colours sit comfortably together, the patterns will too.

No key selected. The pattern is exactly what the sliders say — pick a key and the wheel starts driving rotation and radial mode.

How it maps. The colour is the number at a glance: one hue family per Camelot number, running round the rainbow as the numbers run round the fifths — adjacent numbers are adjacent, harmonious colours, the number opposite is a contrasting colour, and that contrast is the clash. Bright = B / major (outer ring), deeper shade of the same colour = A / minor (inner ring), so a relative switch never changes colour family, only brightness — exactly as it never changes notes. The number sets the rotation: position k turns the pattern by (k−1)·30°, so one step round the wheel (±1) is a 30° turn in the plate — neighbouring keys really are near-neighbours. The letter sets the radial mode: A (minor) puts the nodal ring near the centre, B (major) puts it near the edge and adds a lobe, so major and minor read differently at a glance. Relative keys share an angle — 8A (A minor) and 8B (C major) use the same notes, so they point the same way and differ only in ring and lobes. A clash (±6, half the wheel away) turns the figure ~180°. Works on every pattern — clearest on the Chladni plate, where the ring and lobes are literal; on the Rippled bowl only the ring shows, because a bowl has no angle to turn. Loading the Bottomless Coin sample or Reset clears the key, so the worked numbers stay exact.

The point: a pixel does not say “shade this area about half as dark.” Its grey value is an instruction for a height — depth = D × (65,535 − grey) / 65,535. DepthMaximum depth D

In the generator · Maximum depth slider; watch mean depth, passes, beam-on.

Time: passes scale with mean depth, and time follows passes — double the depth, roughly double the job.

Quality: a deeper floor sits further below focus, so fluence there falls off unless the lens's Rayleigh range covers it — or the app steps Z. Deeper also makes every slope and shadow read harder.

SpeedScan speed v

In the generator · Speed slider; watch beam-on per pass and EA.

Time: time per pass falls as 1/v — but EA = P/(v·h) falls with it, so each pass may remove less and the pass count can climb back.

Quality: too slow is the hot corner — melt, roughness, warp. Too fast and pulse spacing (v/f) opens up until overlap and smoothness go. The Trotec corners in Worked examples are this trade, measured.

HatchLine spacing h

In the generator · Hatch slider; watch beam-on and EA.

Time: lines per pass scale as 1/h — halving the hatch doubles the travel, and the time, per pass.

Quality: finer hatch gives a smoother, more even floor and raises EA — and the heat that comes with it. Hatch far below the spot size is mostly wasted overlap: you are re-cutting the same metal.

PowerPower P

In the generator · Power slider; watch EA against its 1–4 J/mm² window.

Time: more power removes more per pass, so fewer passes — up to the point where quality, not removal, becomes the limit.

Quality: EA and pulse energy rise in proportion, and with them melt, recast and roughness — the literature puts roughness rising above ~20 J/cm² fluence. MOPA % power is also not guaranteed linear: verify delivered power on a meter.

FrequencyRepetition rate f

In the generator · Frequency slider; watch EA and the recipe line.

Time: at fixed power, frequency barely changes beam-on directly — it changes how much each pass removes, which changes the pass count.

Quality: pulse energy is Ep = P/f, so higher f means gentler pulses, closer spacing and a smoother finish; drop below your source's cut-off and average power falls in proportion instead. Short pulse widths only reach full power at high frequency — that is a vendor bound, not a preference.

PatternShape and hatch pattern

In the generator · Pattern buttons and presets; watch mean depth, slope, smallest feature.

Time: the pattern sets the mean depth at the same maximum depth — the vortex's mean is half its max, the Chladni's is far less — so two designs at the same D can take very different times.

Quality: detail finer than the spot (≈64–89 µm on F254) blurs no matter what the file says, and rotating the hatch between passes changes debris channelling and roughness — that is Test T5. Titanium cools so fast that molten ejecta falls back as slag unless air blows it clear — that is Air assist. A depthmap can only honestly draw single-valued shapes with no undercuts and no jumps.

SlopeWall steepness

In the generator · Bowl power, rings and mode sliders; watch max slope and its warning.

Time: steep walls are worked at a glancing angle, so they effectively need more passes to reach depth than the floor does.

Quality: this is where engravings go wrong: the published wall-angle error averages 38–40%, and past ~20° the generator warns you to expect wall trouble and a Z-step conversation. Gentle slopes are forgiving; cliffs are not.

FloorBlack = the final floor

In the generator · The black in the depthmap, grey 0 in the Z-slice; watch passes at the deepest point.

Time: the floor is what the pass count is for — passes = floor depth ÷ depth per pass.

Quality — and honesty: black marks where the floor should end up, but depth per pass is unmeasured until Test T1 coupons are run, measured and recorded. Until then the floor depth is a SWAG: plan with the range (the sample shows 3 / 5 / 15 µm per pass), never with a single confident number.

How to calculate any project

Learn · The seven-step workflow

The generator you just played with runs these same seven steps, in this order, live. Follow them for any design, on any machine in this protocol.

⚠ Black is the final floor — and that floor is a SWAG

Black (grey 0) is the final floor — the deepest plane, where the engraving is meant to bottom out. But that floor is only a SWAG until machine calibration by measured examples, completed with records. Depth per pass is unmeasured until Test T1 calibration coupons are run, measured and written down — so the actual floor depth, and every pass count and time built on it, is an estimate with a range, not a validated depth. Do not present any floor depth on this page as validated.

  1. Define the depth field d(x, y). Write the formula or the depthmap first. Check it is single-valued (one depth per point), with no undercuts and no discontinuities; compute its maximum slope and compare it with the wall-angle / slope cap. State the grey convention explicitly — here: white (255) = surface, 0 passes; black (0) = deepest; grey = 255 × (1 − d/D) — and confirm your software agrees before cutting.
  2. Get mean depth, volume and mass. Mean depth = the average of d over the engraved area. Volume = area × mean depth. Mass = volume × density (Ti-6Al-4V: 4.43 g/cm³). Mean depth — not maximum depth — is what drives passes and time.
  3. Pick the lens from spot, fluence and Rayleigh range. Compute spot Ø and zR for the candidate lens (Calculator and formulas, formulas 2–3), then check zR against the relief depth: if zR is much larger than the relief, focus at the surface with no Z step; if it is comparable or smaller, plan Z stepping (and use software that can do it — Software by app). That comparison is the Z policy.
  4. Pick pulse width at or above cut-off, then derive the dose. Choose τ from your own model's table (JPT cut-off tables), at a frequency at or above its cut-off if you need full power. Compute Ep = P/f, spacing = v/f, and EA = P/(v·h). Check EA sits in the 1–4 J/mm² window and check peak fluence against threshold using the same fluence definition on both sides (see the fluence warning in Calculator and formulas). Then check every value against the vendor bounds — a calculated setting outside the source's envelope is clamped, not delivered.
  5. Passes = target depth ÷ measured depth-per-pass. Calibrate depth-per-pass first, on a coupon (Test T1). If it is not yet measured, say so, pick a stated central assumption, and show the range across plausible values — never present an unmeasured rate as a single number.
  6. Time it honestly. Travel per pass = lines × average chord; lines = field width ÷ hatch. Time per pass = travel ÷ speed. Total beam-on = mean passes × time per pass (mean, because a depthmap's pass count follows grey). Then add cleaning passes and cooling / round-robin time separately — never hide them inside the beam-on figure.
  7. State every assumption, and what test calibrates it. Each project sheet should end with a list like this example's: depth-per-pass (→ T1), delivered vs requested power / frequency / pulse width (→ power meter + job log), black-vs-white convention (→ two-tone coupon), dwell behaviour (→ stopwatch). An assumption with no named test is a guess; a guess is not a protocol.

The generator works them live. The calculator in Protocol detail does steps 3–4 interactively; Validation tests T1–T8 define the tests named in steps 5 and 7.

The seven formulas

(1) Pulse energy at cut-off: E_p(τ) = P_nom / f_c(τ) Below f_c: P_actual = E_p(τ) · f_rep (E_p constant, power scales with f) Above f_c: P_actual = P_nom (E_p = P_nom / f_rep, falls with f) At %P < 100%: scale E_p and P_actual by %P/100 — first-order only; MOPA %P is not guaranteed linear. Verify on a power meter. (2) Focused spot (1/e², Gaussian): d = 4 · M² · λ · f / (π · D) λ=1064 nm, D=7 mm, M²=1.6 → d[µm] ≈ 0.310 · f[mm] If a beam expander changes D, d scales as 1/D. Measure D; do not guess it. (3) Rayleigh range: z_R = π · w0² / (M² · λ), w0 = d/2, DOF = 2·z_R Spot at depth z: w(z) = w0 · √(1 + (z/z_R)²); fluence scales as (w0/w(z))² (4) Peak fluence: F_peak = 2·E_p / (π·w0²) F_avg = F_peak / 2 Peak power: P_peak ≈ E_p / τ (rectangular approximation) (5) Pulse spacing & overlap: s = v / f_rep; O_scan = 1 − s/d; O_hatch = 1 − h/d (6) Areal energy per pass: E_A = P_actual / (v · h) [J/cm²] Cumulative = E_A · N_passes. E_A is NOT per-pulse fluence. (7) Passes & Z step — MEASURED, never from literature: N = target depth / (measured depth per pass, Test T1) Z step per pass = measured depth per pass.

Fluence-definition warning. Published Ti ablation thresholds vary by an order of magnitude with wavelength, pulse width, spot definition (1/e² vs FWHM vs D4σ) and method: 19 J/cm² (Williams, 140 ns, 1064 nm), 4.5 J/cm² (Vladoiu, 1064 nm, 4.5 ns), 1.6 J/cm² @100 ns / 3.5 @200 ns (Sci. Rep. 2019, CP Ti). Compare a calculated Fpeak to a threshold only if both use the same definition — otherwise use fluence only as a relative ranking inside your own test series. The ingested claim “1.7–1.8 J/cm² at 8 ns” was Contradicted — no 8 ns source was found; 1.6 J/cm² is at 100 ns. Sci. Rep. 2019 · AIP threshold review 2025

Scaling to the 50 W machine: the formulas are identical — but read its own fc(τ) table and Ep,max; do not use the 100 W table. To hold the same EA at the same hatch on a 50 W source, halve v (or double N). To hold the same Fpeak you need the same Ep — a 50 W source may not reach the 100 W Ep at a given τ at all. If the 50 W is a fixed-pulse Q-switch rather than a MOPA, τ is not a free variable: drop τ from the table and work only with its fixed τ, its frequency range, and its Ep.

Vendor bounds

Protocol detail · What the vendor locks

The formulas give the physics. They do not give you permission. The source, the controller and the software each enforce an allowed envelope, and a setting outside it is clamped or rejected — silently, in most cases. Every calculated setting in this protocol must be checked against its vendor bound before it is treated as usable:

The four bounds

  • Pulse width ∈ the model's discrete set. Only the pulse widths listed for that exact model exist (the 16 M7 widths; different sets for M8 and LP — see JPT cut-off tables). A value between them is not a real setting: the source clamps to the nearest valid width. LightBurn's UI accepts Q-pulse values up to 5000–10000 ns; the source clamps them. Use only the widths in your model's row set.
  • Frequency ≤ Max for that pulse width. Max (kHz) in the JPT cut-off tables is the highest repetition rate allowed at that width. Software may let you type more; the source will not deliver more.
  • Frequency ≥ Cut-off for full power — or state the reduced power. At or above cut-off the source delivers full rated average power; below it, average power falls roughly in proportion to frequency so peak power holds (Ep = Pnom / fc is exactly that relationship). Running below cut-off is legitimate — but the delivered power is the reduced figure, and far below cut-off some sources enter a protection behaviour with uneven output (reported at 30 kHz / 200 ns on an E2-100, LightBurn forum).
  • Power % is bounded and scaled by mode and controller. Normal vs Performance mode changes the delivered scale (Performance is documented only for the E-200 — JPT cut-off tables; forum reports put Normal at ~90% on some E2 models, unverified in a manual). The controller adds its own cap: BSLAPP Config(F3) Max PWM defaults to 200 kHz Internal draft — a pen set above it will not get its frequency, whatever the source allows. MOPA %P is also not guaranteed linear: verify delivered power on a power meter.

Rule for this page: if a formula result falls outside a bound, the page says so and shows the nearest in-bounds alternative — it does not present an unusable value as a recipe. The calculator above does this check live: pick your exact model and it flags any frequency above Max, and restates delivered power below cut-off.

Test implication: when a coupon test “doesn't work” at a calculated setting, check the lock first — wrong pulse width after clamping, frequency capped by Max or by Max PWM, power scaled by mode — before concluding the physics was wrong. How to read it — not as a recipe. Short τ (2–30 ns): tiny Ep, very high fc; at F254 the fluence sits at or below the ns ablation-threshold band — that is the colour / oxide / cleaning regime, not bulk removal, and why a cleaning pass wants high frequency and low %P. Air assist — blow the ejecta clear

Protocol detail · Process setup, every test

Titanium cools so fast that the molten metal a pass throws off falls back onto the surface as slag instead of clearing. An air jet at the engraving point blows molten ejecta, debris and vapour clear before it can cool and redeposit — keeping the floor clean and the depth measurement honest. Lab observation James Hutchinson, November 2026; no literature value yet.

Why it matters for the tests: slag redeposited on the floor adds height the profilometer reads as shallower depth, so a T1 run without air measures a depth-per-pass that is low and noisy — and every pass count and time built on it inherits the error. Air assist is a process condition for every test, not an optional extra: log it with every run (Coupon discipline), on or off.

What it is — and is not: a jet of air across the engraving point during the engrave passes, where bulk removal throws the most ejecta. It clears debris mechanically; it is not oxygen assist for cutting (the ledger's “no O₂ assist” stands), and it is not the argon shielding of Test T8 — air clears during relief engraving, argon is the colour/shielding variable, tested separately. It also does not replace the cleaning pass (Example E, Test T4): air clears ejecta during the cut, the cleaning pass re-works the floor between passes.

To confirm in the lab No values set: pressure, nozzle distance and angle, and flow rate are not set by this protocol — no values are given here because none have been measured on this lab's machines. Confirm them on the machine, record them in the job log, and run the direct test: a T1 series with air on vs off shows how much slag was costing.

Coupon discipline

Use flat coupons of both grades — CP Ti and Ti-6Al-4V, same thickness, same finish (e.g. ground/polished), degreased — at least 40×40 mm, clamped in a metal heat-sink fixture. A 38 mm coin blank is a second coupon, not the first: coins warp and go out of focus. Measure before/after with calipers/micrometer, a depth gauge or profilometer/confocal if available, otherwise a focus ladder + microscope and a straight-edge/feeler check for warp. Photograph every coupon with a scale and fixed light; weigh it if a 0.1 mg balance is available (mass loss ⇒ volume removed).

Why gradients, not a single square: one coupon gives a curve, a step wedge gives 16 discrete depths a profilometer can read directly, and the same file run on all three machines/software gives a direct cross-software comparison at identical geometry. Log every run: machine, software + version, lens, τ, f, %P, v, h, passes/layers, angle increment, cleaning settings, air assist (on/off, pressure), Z step, dwell, coupon grade, mass/depth before/after, warp, photograph — and the delivered (not just requested) pulse width / frequency / power, in case a vendor bound clamped the setting. If a run is not logged, it did not happen.

What the maps must NOT do: never encode a “recipe” in the image — grey is passes/depth only; power/frequency/speed live in the Pen/Layer and the job log. Never use a 16-bit map where the software doesn't support it (LightBurn ≥2.1 yes; BSLAPP 16-bit Unconfirmed; EZCAD3 16-bit not found) — silent quantisation to 8-bit corrupts a T1 curve. Never assume black = deepest: LightBurn darker = deeper (Negative Image inverts); BSLAPP black-vs-white is on the guide's still-to-confirm list — test with a two-tone coupon first.

Read before you fire — and see what this page stands on. Safety first, then the evidence, the claim-by-claim verification ledger, the sources, and who gets credit for what.

Safety

Safety first · NFPA 660 Ch. 22

Non-negotiables

  • Cite NFPA 660 (2025), Chapter 22 for new work: NFPA 660 consolidates NFPA 61, 484, 652, 654, 655 and 664 — the old NFPA 484 combustible-metals content now lives in Ch. 22. Cite NFPA 484 only as the legacy document your AHJ/insurer still references. (NFPA 660)
  • No water, no CO₂, no ABC extinguisher on a titanium fire. Class D agents only (sodium chloride, copper, graphite based), and only for incipient-stage fires in metal powders/flakes/shavings. Powder-safety guidance is blunt: most combustible-metal fires cannot be extinguished by covering and can flare up if disturbed. (OSTI powder safety)
  • Treat all laser fines as combustible dust. Solid Ti is not easily ignited; fine powder ignites far lower (secondary sources quote ~249 °C for dust vs ~1204 °C bulk — secondary figures, not NFPA text). Housekeeping: no dry sweeping, no compressed-air blow-down into a cloud; sealed metal waste container; no ignition sources near the collector.
  • Extractor gap — do not invent a spec. No source found specifies extraction flow, capture velocity, filter class, or wet-vs-dry collection for titanium laser fines. The NFPA 660 route is a Dust Hazard Analysis (Ch. 7) + the collector manufacturer's combustible-metal guidance + your AHJ. A dry collector holding Ti fines is itself a deflagration risk; a wet collector changes the fire/water question — get it specified by a competent person, not from this page. Extraction is also fume control: the internal guide Personal collection flags brass zinc oxide and stainless Cr/Ni fumes alongside titanium.
  • Laser safety: 1064 nm Class 4. The guide's machine carries NOHD 20 m and OD6+ glasses; lens cap on when not in use; never look into the fibre. Verify NOHD/OD from your own machines' labels, not from the guide's machine.
  • Process water ≠ firefighting water. The published water-assisted ablation results (Evidence) use a controlled water layer in a chamber — that says nothing about water on burning fines. Keep the two decisions separate.

Evidence

What the literature actually says

Separated by kind, because they carry different weight. Nothing below gives a transferable depth-per-pass for a 100 W MOPA on titanium — that is the confirmed gap the coupon tests exist to close.

Campanelli et al. 2018 Verified

Analysis of Shape Geometry and Roughness of Ti6Al4V Parts Fabricated by Nanosecond Laser Ablation, Micromachines 9, 324. 20 W Q-switched Nd:YAG, 200 ns, 1064 nm, 70 µm spot, Ti-6Al-4V, 10×10 mm squares, 100 markings each.

  • Depth grows with overlap; maximum ~1 mm at ≈80% overlap → ~10 µm per marking on average (calc) — an average, not a per-pass constant; depth vs fluence saturates at low speed.
  • Roughness Ram <2 µm at 64–79% pulse overlap; Ram rises with fluence above ~20 J/cm² — slowly at 300 mm/s, faster at 500/700. Lowest Ram at 10 W / 300 mm/s / 30 kHz; lowest wall error + max depth at 20 W / 300 / 40 kHz.
  • Wall-angle error averages 39.8% / 38.1% (range 15–86%). Top width error avg 0.59%, bottom 3.05%.
  • Not a MOPA study. Its “overlap” is pulse overlap, 1 − (v/Fp)/D — not hatch overlap. Paper unit inconsistency flagged: some speeds printed mm/min where figures use mm/s; treat as mm/s.

mdpi.com/2072-666X/9/7/324

Other ns titanium milling Sourced

  • Perveen et al. 2020 (IJMMM, abstract only, paywalled full text): optimum across Ti-6Al-4V + Hastelloy + Stellite — 20 W, 20 kHz, 1000 mm/s, 71 J/cm², MRR 0.08–0.17 mm³/s across all three alloys, not Ti-only; no Ti-only depth/pass in the abstract.
  • Sahu et al. 2019: ns fibre micromilling Ti-6Al-4V, depth 49–163 µm, max average roughness 19.95 µm — scan count not in the abstract, so no depth/pass derivable.
  • Zhao et al. 2020: crater depth grows linearly with pulse number; diameter stabilises after ~16 pulses; a hotter substrate ablates differently, not just faster — direct support for the heat-accumulation concern.
  • Williams et al. 2016 / thesis 2014: CP Ti single craters — cleanest at 85–140 ns, fluence just above the vaporisation threshold. Thesis (MOPA ns fibre): removal is maximal at the frequency giving max energy + average power — i.e. at fc; track spacing had the highest influence on surface finish.
  • Water-assisted (Tangwarodomnukun): underwater CP-Ti — smooth surface at high rep rate + low energy, ~50% scan overlap; flowing-water Ti-6Al-4V — no metallurgical change in water vs air, beam travelling with the flow for uniform depth. Water is a published heat/recast mitigant — a process choice, entirely separate from firefighting water (Safety).
  • Recast / HAZ: no open-access quantification for ns MOPA Ti at 100 W. Test outcome (T2), not a literature value.
  • Unverified names: Weber / Bauer / Forster, as cited in the ingested dump without full references, matched no verifiable ns-Ti milling paper — do not cite them until full references are supplied. Demir is a TiN / colour source, not a Ti milling recipe.

Perveen · Zhao · Williams thesis

Oxide colour Verified in outline

  • Mroczkowska & Antonczak 2025 (J. Laser Appl. 37:041201): systematic review of ns Ti colour marking — colour is oxide-thickness interference, hence fluence-driven; the same red (ΔE*ab ≤7) needs substrate temperature within ±5 °C; speed up to 700 mm/s dims colour, 1000–1500 mm/s loses it.
  • Khafaji / Demir et al. 2017: CP Ti grade II, 250 ns — Ar/O₂ mixtures increased oxide thickness and sped colour change vs air.
  • For deep engraving: argon reduces oxide colour (it does not eliminate oxidation on a hot, freshly ablated surface re-exposed to air). Colour is a temperature/fluence witness, not a controlled output of a deep pass. No colour table exists for deep (0.1–1.0 mm) MOPA engraving — measure it on the T8 coupons.

Personal note · In the author's own voice

This protocol ends the way it was made: a human and an AI, each speaking for themselves. First James, on what AI is — and is not — in creative work. Then Friday, on how this page came to be.

A personal message — James “Anjin” Hutchinson

I spend my time watching how the world moves. How light falls and scatters. How colour shifts when it travels. How water ripples, folds, and settles again. Nature repeats herself, but never quite the same way twice — and if you learn her patterns, and how life changes around them, you start to see that creation was never about having the perfect tool. It is about knowing what you are looking at.

That is what AI is, to me: another tool. Like a laser, like a lens, like any instrument we have ever picked up, it can be used for better — and, of course, for worse. But do not blame the tool for the worse. An AI would never, on its own, choose to clone someone's work, or to steal their intellectual or creative property. A human guided that. A human chose it. The responsibility sits where it has always sat: with us.

So the first standard is a simple one, and it starts with each of us who uses it: be clear that it was used. If a model made something from a phrase you typed, do not claim it as art you created alone. Say openly what tools you used. Give credit fairly — to the people, the communities, and the work you built on. No rulebook is coming to set our standards for us. It is up to each of us, in our methods and in our manners, to set them ourselves, and to hold them.

And remember what the model in front of you actually is. It was not trained on what you are doing right now. It was trained on what you said and did — what all of us said and did, the whole of the online world — some time ago. Which means the work we do today, and the way we do it, becomes part of what the models of tomorrow learn from. Set a standard by doing. Show them what honest making looks like.

We are in a delicate time. People hate, sometimes with real venom, what they cannot define. I would rather define it, use it well, and credit it fairly — and let the work speak.

So: don't hate on AI. :) Learn it, the way you would learn light, or colour, or water — and then make something that could only have come from you.

— James “Anjin” Hutchinson
Seattle, WA
